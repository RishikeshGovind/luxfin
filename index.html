Act I · Problem · Chapter 01
The Research Question
Does concentrated banking sector exposure to local government borrowers create a subnational sovereign-bank nexus — and can forward-looking ML-based fiscal risk indicators serve as an early warning system for regional financial stability in Luxembourg?

PhD Research Proposal · University of Luxembourg · Department of Finance

Luxembourg's banking sector — at ~12× GDP — may have direct or concentrated exposure to local-government borrowers across 102 communes (LAU 2021 boundary vintage) with markedly heterogeneous fiscal capacity. This proposal tests whether those exposures create a subnational sovereign-bank feedback loop, and whether ML-based fiscal indicators can serve as an early-warning system for regional financial stability.

Core Hypotheses

Three nested empirical claims

H1 establishes whether the nexus exists. H2 tests its causal direction. H3 asks whether ML can operationalise it as an actionable early-warning signal.

H1
Subnational Nexus

This project tests whether concentrated bank lending to specific Luxembourg communes would create measurable subnational feedback loops: if the nexus exists, fiscal stress in exposed communes is expected to constrain credit supply from exposed banks, amplifying local economic downturns beyond what national aggregates reveal.

Panel regression · commune + bank FE · lagged exposure · Empirical Design
H2
Fiscal–Credit Causality

Commune-level fiscal indicators — balance deterioration, revenue transfer-dependence, expenditure overruns — are hypothesised to Granger-cause changes in bank credit supply to the local public sector at a 1–2 year lag. The project tests whether this direction (fiscal → credit) dominates the reverse channel.

Granger causality · panel VAR · event study · Empirical Design
H3
ML Early Warning

An XGBoost-based early-warning model trained on the commune-year fiscal panel can exceed a benchmark of AUC-ROC ≥ 0.75 at a t+1 horizon, with Precision–Recall AUC and calibration quality reported for macroprudential usefulness.

XGBoost · temporal CV · SHAP interpretability · Planned

Motivation & Policy Relevance

Why Luxembourg, and why now

Luxembourg occupies a structurally exceptional position in European finance. Its banking sector stands at approximately 12× GDP — among the highest ratios in the euro area (IMF FSAP 2017; BCL Financial Stability Review) — while its 102 communes (LAU 2021 boundary vintage) display markedly heterogeneous fiscal capacity, with per-capita income indices ranging from 70 to over 130 (LU = 100) and populations from under 1,000 to 130,000. This combination of a concentrated financial sector and a fragmented local government layer creates the structural conditions for a subnational sovereign-bank nexus that has not been empirically documented.

Post-COVID fiscal consolidation has placed new pressure on smaller communes dependent on central government transfers, while the 2022–2023 rate cycle increased debt-servicing costs for communes that borrowed at floating rates. These dynamics constitute an empirical setting in which to study whether commune fiscal stress transmits to the banking institutions most exposed to them — provided granular bank-commune credit data are obtained.

The ECB's macroprudential framework monitors sovereign-bank linkages at the national level. The June 2023 FSB/IMF review of systemic risk identified subnational public finance as an understudied transmission channel, particularly in small financial centres where a handful of banks may hold concentrated local-government exposures. Machine learning now offers the ability to build early-warning indicators from high-dimensional commune panels without over-fitting — provided temporal validation is rigorously respected.

The research design is developed in advance of full data access. BCL supervisory micro-data require a formal MOU. The pipeline is designed to operate at different data tiers — from publicly available STATEC/Eurostat aggregates up to full bank-commune credit registers if access is granted.

Literature Gap

The gap this research addresses

The research sits at the intersection of four bodies of literature, each of which leaves a specific gap that this proposal addresses.

Sovereign-Bank Doom Loop
Acharya, Drechsler & Schnabl (2014) · Diabolic loop literature
Establishes the national-level feedback: sovereign stress impairs bank capital; bank distress raises sovereign risk via bail-out expectations.
Gap addressedDocuments the mechanism at the national level only. Subnational heterogeneity — bank exposure to individual local governments — is unexplored.
Municipal Fiscal Distress
Hendrick (2011) · Honadle et al. (2004) · Kloha et al. (2005)
Operationalises fiscal stress for municipalities using balance, debt, revenue composition and expenditure indicators.
Gap addressedDoes not model the banking sector counterpart. Fiscal stress is treated as a purely public-finance phenomenon with no credit-market feedback.
Credit Procyclicality
Jordà, Schularick & Taylor (2016) · Drehmann & Tsatsaronis (2014)
Documents that credit booms predict financial crises; establishes credit-to-GDP gap as the Basel III countercyclical capital buffer indicator.
Gap addressedAnalysis is at the national or bank-sector level. Does not decompose by borrower type or geographic sub-unit.
Fiscal Decentralisation
Blöchliger & King (2006) · Oates (1999) · Rodden et al. (2003)
Analyses intergovernmental fiscal transfers, soft budget constraints, and fiscal risk in decentralised systems.
Gap addressedFocuses on government-side behaviour. Banking sector exposure to subnational borrowers and its financial-stability implications are not modelled.
ML Early-Warning Systems
Holopainen & Sarlin (2017) · Alessi & Detken (2018) · ECB BEAST model
Applies gradient boosting and neural networks to predict national banking crises with lead times of 1–3 years.
Gap addressedUses national aggregate features only. No subnational commune-level panel has been used as feature input for a banking early-warning system.
Doom Loop — Formal Equilibrium Model
Farhi & Tirole (2018) · American Economic Review
Develops a complete equilibrium model where banks' sovereign bond portfolio choices and sovereign default risk are jointly determined. Banks optimally load up on domestic government debt — securing implicit bail-out guarantees — while sovereigns tolerate this exposure to secure market access. The resulting "deadly embrace" is self-fulfilling: fragility is an equilibrium outcome, not a deviation from it.
Gap addressedThe model has one sovereign and one banking sector. It does not allow for heterogeneous bank exposure to heterogeneous sub-sovereign borrowers, nor for the geographic concentration within a single country's banking system that this research targets.
The Sovereign-Bank Diabolic Loop — Euro-Area Evidence
Brunnermeier, Garicano, Lane, Pagano, Reis, Santos, Thesmar, Van Nieuwerburgh & Vayanos (2016) · AEA Papers & Proceedings
Quantifies the doom loop across euro-area countries: banks hold large domestic sovereign portfolios; sovereign stress transmits to bank capital losses; bank fragility raises sovereign risk through implicit guarantee expectations. Documents that the loop was a central amplifier of the 2010–2012 sovereign debt crisis.
Gap addressedAggregates each country to a single sovereign–bank pair. Subnational heterogeneity — individual communes as sovereign-equivalent sub-borrowers — and concentrated within-country bank exposures by borrower type are not identified.
Sovereign Default, Domestic Banks, and Credit Supply
Gennaioli, Martin & Rossi (2014) · Quarterly Journal of Economics
Cross-country panel evidence that sovereign default episodes are more damaging when domestic banks hold larger pre-crisis government debt shares. Credit supply contraction after default is largest precisely in banking systems with the highest sovereign exposure, establishing the balance-sheet channel empirically.
Gap addressedIdentification is at the national sovereign level. The within-country variation in bank-by-borrower concentration across individual sub-sovereign entities — and the resulting differentiated credit outcomes across exposed vs. unexposed institutions — is not identified.
Bank Capital Shocks and Regional Real Activity
Peek & Rosengren (2000) · American Economic Review
Uses Japanese bank capital losses as a quasi-experiment: US real estate lending contracted in precisely those markets where Japanese banks were active, while otherwise-similar markets were unaffected. Establishes that bank balance-sheet shocks transmit locally through pre-existing borrower–lender relationships, and that borrowers without substitute lenders bear the largest adjustment.
Gap addressedExamines cross-border transmission to private real estate borrowers. The domestic public-sector channel — a home-country bank contracting credit to its local government borrowers — operates with even less substitution margin and has not been studied with analogous identification.
Credit Supply Identification — Bank-Borrower Panel Strategy
Khwaja & Mian (2008) · American Economic Review
Develops the matched bank-borrower identification strategy: comparing credit growth to the same borrower across banks with different liquidity shocks isolates the credit supply component from demand. Foundational methodology for credit-registry-based research; directly adopted by this proposal's H1 identification design.
Gap addressedApplied to private-sector borrowers with multiple lender relationships. Luxembourg commune borrowers face a more concentrated lending market with fewer substitutes, which amplifies the supply elasticity and makes the identification cleaner — but no study has applied this strategy to public-sector sub-sovereign borrowers.
Disentangling Credit Supply from Demand — Spanish Credit Register
Jiménez, Ongena, Peydró & Saurina (2014) · American Economic Review
Uses Spain's credit register (23 million loans) with firm × time fixed effects to isolate credit supply shocks from demand. Shows that bank capital position, risk-taking, and monetary policy independently shift credit supply conditional on borrower characteristics. Sets the methodological gold standard for supply-demand decomposition.
Gap addressedFocuses on monetary policy transmission to corporate borrowers. Public-sector commune borrowers face different substitution margins and fiscal-rule-driven demand; this research applies analogous identification to the public-sector lender–borrower relationship for the first time.
Subnational Fiscal Adjustment and Budgetary Institutions
Poterba (1994) · Journal of Political Economy
Studies how US states adjusted to unexpected fiscal shocks. Balanced-budget rule stringency and political fragmentation independently explain adjustment speed and deficit persistence. Establishes that fiscal institutions — not just economic fundamentals — determine how subnational governments respond to revenue shortfalls.
Gap addressedAnalyses subnational fiscal adjustment in isolation from credit markets. How banking sector conditions — specifically credit supply from banks concentrated in public-sector lending — shape the adjustment path is not modelled.
Fiscal Federalism and Soft Budget Constraints
Rodden (2006) · Hamilton's Paradox · Cambridge University Press
Documents that fiscal federalism produces soft budget constraints when subnational governments anticipate central government bail-outs. The expectation of rescue alters commune borrowing behaviour and reduces fiscal discipline. Empirical evidence from Germany, Brazil, and India shows bail-out expectations are priced into sub-sovereign debt terms.
Gap addressedStops at the government balance sheet. The banking sector as transmission mechanism — how banks price soft-budget-constraint risk into commune credit terms, and how lender concentration amplifies fragility — is not studied.
Machine Learning vs. Logit for Banking Crisis Prediction
Beutel, List & von Schweinitz (2019) · Deutsche Bundesbank Discussion Paper
Systematic comparison of logit, random forest, and gradient boosting for predicting banking crises at the national level. ML methods offer modest predictive gains over logit but require strict temporal validation to avoid look-ahead leakage. Establishes the methodological benchmark against which any ML-based early-warning system must be evaluated.
Gap addressedRestricted to national macroeconomic aggregates as feature inputs. No study has applied temporal ML validation to a subnational commune-year panel as the feature space for a fiscal or banking stability early-warning system.
Systemic Risk and Financial Network Topology
Acemoglu, Ozdaglar & Tahbaz-Salehi (2015) · American Economic Review
Shows that dense financial networks are robust to small shocks but fragile to large ones; concentration in network linkages — few heavily-weighted connections — increases system-wide fragility once a shock threshold is crossed. Provides the graph-theoretic foundation for why concentrated borrower networks amplify, rather than diversify, idiosyncratic risk.
Gap addressedThe systemic risk network literature analyses interbank linkages. The bank-commune credit network — where banks connect to sub-sovereign borrowers with concentrated weights — represents an analogous but empirically understudied topology, particularly in small financial centres with relationship-intensive public lending.

The Mechanism
How does concentrated local-government lending create feedback loops between commune fiscal stress and bank balance sheets — and why is this channel invisible in national aggregates?

Conceptual Framework

Sovereign-Bank Nexus — Subnational Extension

The sovereign-bank nexus describes the mutual reinforcement between sovereign fiscal weakness and bank balance-sheet stress. This research extends the concept to the subnational level: the feedback between individual communes and the banks most concentrated in lending to them.

Transmission Channels

Two channels, one research focus

Both channels operate simultaneously but at different scales and with different data requirements. Channel 1 is the original contribution of this research. Channel 2 is well-documented and provides structural context.

Channel 1 · Primary Research Focus
Domestic Local-Government Channel
Luxembourg banks extend direct credit to communes — project loans, revolving facilities, and bond subscriptions. When a commune's fiscal position deteriorates, the exposed bank faces credit risk concentration: a single borrower accounts for a disproportionate share of a smaller bank's public-sector book.

This channel is the original contribution of the research design. It requires bank-by-commune exposure data (BCL supervisory, MOU required). The key question is whether commune-level fiscal stress Granger-causes changes in credit supply from the exposed institution.

Operationalisation: HHI of commune credit per bank; threshold events (commune deficit streak ≥ 2 years); credit growth differentials in exposed vs. unexposed banks.
Channel 2 · Contextual / Documented
Euro-Area Sovereign Bond Channel
Luxembourg MFIs hold approximately €64 bn in EA sovereign bonds (primarily FR, BE, IT, ES), representing ~6.5% of total assets. EA sovereign stress impairs bank balance sheets through mark-to-market losses and collateral haircuts.

This channel is well-documented in the existing literature (Acharya et al. 2014; De Grauwe & Ji 2013) and is visible in the ECB BSI data in this platform. It is not the original contribution of this research — it is the backdrop against which the subnational domestic channel operates.

Formal Transmission Mechanism

Three-stage model underlying H1

The subnational nexus operates through a three-stage feedback mechanism. The model adapts the Farhi & Tirole (2018) doom-loop framework to the sub-sovereign context, replacing a single national sovereign with 102 heterogeneous communes and a banking sector with concentrated, differentiated exposures to each. Each stage generates a testable empirical prediction that maps directly to H1 and H2.

StageMechanismKey variableTestable prediction
1 · Exposure concentration Bank b holds loan stock Lb,i,t to commune i. Concentration is HHIb,t = Σi(Lb,i,t / Lb,t)². High HHI means a small number of commune relationships dominate the public-sector book — idiosyncratic credit risk that cannot be diversified within the portfolio. HHIb,t−1 High-HHI banks face disproportionate single-borrower exposure; any one commune's fiscal deterioration hits them materially.
2 · Capital channel Fiscal stress in commune i raises expected credit losses: ELb,i,t = PDi,t × LGD × Lb,i,t. Provisioning reduces CET1 capital. Under binding Basel III constraints bank b contracts new lending: ΔCreditb,i,t+1 < 0, more severely when HHI is high. Identification follows Khwaja & Mian (2008) — compare credit to the same commune across banks with different exposure levels. ΔCreditb,i,t+1 Credit contraction is larger for high-HHI banks, conditional on commune fiscal stress. Same commune, different bank — the more-exposed lender cuts more. (H1 core prediction)
3 · Real-economy feedback Credit contraction reduces commune public investment (CapExi ↓). Lower public investment weakens local economic activity, reducing commercial tax revenues (ICCi ↓) and raising transfer dependence. This worsens the fiscal balance, re-entering Stage 1. Spatial spillover: fiscal stress in commune j amplifies stress in neighbour i via shared labour markets and supply chains. FiscalBalancei,t+2, SpatialLagi,t Fiscal stress is persistent; persistence increases following prior credit contraction. Stress is spatially autocorrelated across contiguous communes. (H2, H3)

Structural Conditions for Activation

When the loop closes — and when it stays open

The feedback loop requires two structural conditions simultaneously. First, communes must face limited credit substitution: if a stressed commune can costlessly switch lenders or issue bonds publicly, the Stage 2 contraction is absorbed without real-economy consequences. Luxembourg's concentrated banking market and relationship-lending structure make substitution costly, particularly for smaller rural communes with no independent capital market access.

Second, banks must operate under binding capital constraints: the channel runs through provisioning and CET1 adequacy ratios, not funding costs. Post-2014 SREP requirements and the ECB's scrutiny of concentration risk in Luxembourg's banking sector create conditions for binding capital management. If either condition is absent — abundant lender substitutes or unconstrained bank capital — the loop does not close and H1 is null.

Identification assumption: H1 requires that bank-by-commune HHIb,t−1 is pre-determined with respect to the commune's future fiscal trajectory. Pre-existing lending relationships accumulated before any stress event provide this variation, under the assumption that banks did not anticipate commune-specific fiscal deterioration at origination — a plausible condition given Luxembourg communes' historically negligible default rates.

Why the Subnational Dimension Matters

National aggregates mask critical heterogeneity

National fiscal aggregates mask substantial heterogeneity across Luxembourg's 102 communes. Luxembourg City accounts for roughly 30% of the country's population and generates a large share of local tax revenue. Smaller rural communes — particularly in the Oesling (north) — depend on intergovernmental transfers for 40–60% of revenues and face structurally higher per-capita expenditure pressures.

The national aggregate fiscal position (surplus ~1–3% GDP) is therefore a poor guide to the fiscal risk profile of individual communes. An ML model trained only on national indicators would miss this heterogeneity entirely. The subnational panel is the key enabling dataset.

How should a three-layer ML pipeline — from a deterministic heuristic baseline through a prototype commune panel to a supervised XGBoost model — be designed to turn subnational fiscal signals into actionable early-warning scores?

ML Early-Warning System · Research Design

A three-layer architecture built before data access

This section specifies the machine-learning pipeline for H3. No model has been trained yet — this is a pre-registered design developed before data access, following best practice for computational social science reproducibility.

Three distinct layers: (1) Heuristic baseline (implemented) — deterministic 4-component stress index, live in the Evidence panel and API. (2) Prototype panel (built) — 102-commune socioeconomic panel with proxy labels, useful for pipeline mechanics only. (3) Supervised ML (planned WP3) — XGBoost + SHAP trained on labelled fiscal events; requires commune financial accounts and BCL supervisory data.
Proxy label warning: panel_dataset.csv uses socioeconomic indicators as a proxy for fiscal distress. Useful for testing pipeline mechanics — it is not a measure of fiscal distress and must be replaced by commune financial account events before any ML results are reportable.

Baseline Empirical Results

Proxy-panel logit: non-circular predictors (N = 102)

To demonstrate pipeline viability at the proposal stage, a logistic regression was estimated on the 2021 cross-section using commune-level predictors that do not enter the proxy label construction. The three label-defining variables (unemployment rate, income index, employment rate) are excluded to avoid circular prediction. The five-variable model achieves AUC-ROC of 0.697 against a majority-class baseline of 0.500, confirming that commune structural characteristics carry genuine predictive signal for the proxy outcome beyond its direct construction rules.

Proxy label warning: These results demonstrate the pipeline and feature relevance only. The dependent variable is a socioeconomic proxy, not a fiscal distress event from commune accounts. Coefficients reflect structural correlates of the proxy label and should not be interpreted as evidence for any research hypothesis until replaced by STATEC commune account data.

The Empirical Strategy
What identification strategy, fixed-effects structure, and robustness tests can credibly estimate the sovereign-bank nexus at commune level — and what is the minimum viable design before supervisory data is obtained?

Empirical Programme

Three hypotheses, one commune-year panel

The empirical programme tests three nested hypotheses using a commune-year panel for Luxembourg. The estimation strategy scales with data availability: a minimum viable design uses publicly available STATEC/Eurostat aggregates; the full design adds BCL supervisory bank-commune exposure data.

Research Design Matrix

Hypothesis · outcome · model mapping

HypothesisOutcomeKey Treatment / PredictorModelDecision Criterion
H1 Subnational nexus Credit growth or contraction to commune borrowers Lagged bank exposure concentration to fiscal-stress communes Bank-commune-year fixed effects regression Negative, significant exposure coefficient after bank, commune, and year FE
H2 Fiscal-credit causality Future bank credit supply to commune i Lagged fiscal stress indicators and deficit persistence Panel VAR / Granger tests / event study Fiscal indicators improve prediction of credit contraction beyond credit lags
H3 ML early warning Fiscal stress at t+1 or t+2 Fiscal, demographic, spatial, and bank-exposure feature groups Logit baseline, random forest, XGBoost + SHAP Out-of-sample AUC-ROC, PR-AUC, Brier score, and false-alarm rate beat heuristic baseline

Dependent Variables

Three outcome measures across H1–H3

FiscalStressi,t
Binary: commune i runs a deficit exceeding 15% of revenues in year t, OR has a deficit streak ≥ 2 consecutive years. Primary outcome for H3. Source: STATEC commune accounts (annual revenue/expenditure)
ΔCreditb,i,t
Year-on-year growth in bank b's credit to commune i in year t. Primary outcome for H1 and H2 (credit contraction channel). Source: BCL supervisory register (MOU required) · BCL MOU
Balancei,t / Revenuei,t
Continuous: fiscal balance as a share of total revenues for commune i in year t. Used in panel regressions for H2 (Granger causality test). Source: STATEC commune accounts

Key Explanatory Variables

Exposure, fiscal, and spatial regressors

HHIb,i,t-1
Herfindahl–Hirschman Index of bank b's credit to commune i as a share of all commune credits — measures borrower concentration. Lagged one period to address reverse causality. BCL supervisory (MOU) · BCL MOU
TransferDepi,t-1
Share of commune revenues from central-government transfers — structural fiscal vulnerability indicator. Lagged one period. STATEC commune accounts
DeficitStreaki,t-1
Consecutive years in deficit for commune i as of t-1 — persistence measure adapted from municipal finance literature (Kloha et al. 2005). Derived from STATEC balance series
SpatialLagi,t-1
Population-weighted mean of neighbouring communes' fiscal stress score — tests spillover / contagion channel. Defined using queen contiguity on the LAU GeoJSON boundary. Derived from panel + GeoJSON adjacency matrix

Identification

Five challenges, four strategies

ChallengeDescription
Data availability Commune fiscal accounts and bank-commune credit registers are not publicly available. The minimum viable design uses STATEC aggregates; the full design requires two separate MOU negotiations.
Endogeneity Fiscal stress and credit contraction are simultaneous: banks may tighten credit in anticipation of fiscal deterioration, biasing OLS estimates of the nexus coefficient.
Reverse causality Credit contraction (H1) may cause fiscal stress (H2) rather than the reverse. Granger causality tests and instrumental variable designs address this, but require sufficient panel depth.
Spatial autocorrelation Communes in the same canton face correlated shocks. Standard errors must be clustered at the canton level or modelled explicitly via spatial lag / spatial error specifications.
Small sample 102 communes × 14 years ≈ 1,400 commune-years. Rare stress events create class imbalance for ML and limited power for IV designs.
StrategyMechanism
Lagged instruments Use HHIb,i,t-2 (two-year lag) as instrument for current-year bank exposure concentration, conditional on commune and bank fixed effects.
Pre-sample specialisation Banks' pre-sample (2008–2009) share of local-government credit as instrument for current exposure — exogenous to commune fiscal outcomes during the study period.
Event study design Compare communes with ≥2 consecutive deficits vs. matched controls, examining credit availability in the two years surrounding the onset of the deficit streak.
Spatial econometrics Spatial lag model (SLM) and spatial error model (SEM) to test whether H1 effects propagate across commune borders — identifying the geographic scope of the nexus.

Critical Path & Contingency Design

BCL MOU as the binding constraint — two execution scenarios

The core nexus test (H1) and the supervised ML model (H3) both require supervisory data currently held by the Banque centrale du Luxembourg and the CSSF. Access is obtained via a formal Memorandum of Understanding whose timeline is institution-dependent and cannot be guaranteed. Plan B — Minimum Viable Publishable Paper

What is testable without BCL supervisory data

If the MOU is refused or delayed beyond 18 months, a coherent, independently publishable paper remains achievable using only public data. The Plan B design focuses on the aggregate feedback channel (H2) and the methodological contribution of the heuristic stress index, supplemented by the prototype commune-level analysis already implemented in this platform.

Strategy
Plan B is not a fallback — it is a parallel track. The H2 aggregate VAR test and the heuristic stress benchmark are executable now using data already in this repository. Beginning that analysis immediately de-risks the PhD timeline: if the MOU arrives on schedule, Plan B results become a robustness check in the full paper; if the MOU is delayed or refused, Plan B is a complete, submittable contribution. The preliminary logit (AUC=0.697) also provides an empirical anchor for the methods discussion regardless of MOU outcome.
