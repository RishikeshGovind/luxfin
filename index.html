H Core Hypotheses

§1 Motivation & Policy Relevance

Why Luxembourg?

Luxembourg occupies a structurally exceptional position in European finance. Its banking sector stands at approximately 12× GDP — the largest ratio in the euro area — while its 102 communes display markedly heterogeneous fiscal capacity, with per-capita income indices ranging from 70 to over 130 (LU = 100) and populations from under 1,000 to 130,000. This combination of a concentrated financial sector and a fragmented local government layer creates the conditions for a subnational sovereign-bank nexus that has not been empirically documented.

Post-COVID fiscal consolidation has placed new pressure on smaller communes dependent on central government transfers, while the 2022–2023 rate cycle increased debt-servicing costs for communes that borrowed at floating rates. These dynamics create a natural quasi-experiment to study whether commune fiscal stress transmits to the banking institutions most exposed to them.

Why Now?

The ECB's macroprudential framework monitors sovereign-bank linkages at the national level. The June 2023 FSB/IMF review of systemic risk identified subnational public finance as an understudied transmission channel, particularly in small financial centres where a handful of banks may hold concentrated local-government exposures.

Machine learning techniques now offer the ability to build early-warning indicators from high-dimensional commune panels without over-fitting — provided temporal validation is rigorously respected. No such system exists for Luxembourg's subnational public finance. The BCL and CSSF supervisory data, if accessible via MOU, would make this one of the most granular bank-commune nexus studies in the academic literature.

ℹ The research design is developed in advance of full data access. BCL supervisory micro-data require a formal MOU. The empirical pipeline is designed to operate at different data tiers — from publicly available STATEC / Eurostat aggregates up to full bank-commune credit registers if access is granted.

§2 Literature Gap

The research sits at the intersection of four bodies of literature, each of which leaves a specific gap that this proposal addresses.

Sovereign-Bank Doom Loop
Acharya, Drechsler & Schnabl (2014) · Diabolic loop literature
Establishes the national-level feedback: sovereign stress impairs bank capital; bank distress raises sovereign risk via bail-out expectations.
Documents the mechanism at the national level only. Subnational heterogeneity — bank exposure to individual local governments — is unexplored.
Municipal Fiscal Distress
Hendrick (2011) · Honadle et al. (2004) · Kloha et al. (2005)
Operationalises fiscal stress for municipalities using balance, debt, revenue composition and expenditure indicators. Validates early-warning scoring rules.
Does not model the banking sector counterpart. Fiscal stress is treated as a purely public-finance phenomenon with no credit-market feedback.
Credit Procyclicality
Jordà, Schularick & Taylor (2016) · Drehmann & Tsatsaronis (2014)
Documents that credit booms predict financial crises; establishes credit-to-GDP gap as the Basel III countercyclical capital buffer indicator.
Analysis is at the national or bank-sector level. Does not decompose by borrower type (private vs. local government) or by geographic sub-unit.
Fiscal Decentralisation
Blöchliger & King (2006) · Oates (1999) · Rodden et al. (2003)
Analyses intergovernmental fiscal transfers, soft budget constraints, and fiscal risk in decentralised systems.
Focuses on government-side behaviour. The banking sector exposure to subnational borrowers and its financial-stability implications are not modelled.
ML Early-Warning Systems
Holopainen & Sarlin (2017) · Alessi & Detken (2018) · ECB BEAST model
Applies gradient boosting, neural networks and SHAP to predict national banking crises and systemic risk events with lead times of 1–3 years.
Uses national aggregate features (credit-to-GDP, house prices, CA deficit). No subnational commune-level panel has been used as feature input for a banking EWS.

§3 Sovereign-Bank Nexus — Conceptual Framework

The sovereign-bank nexus describes the mutual reinforcement between sovereign fiscal weakness and bank balance-sheet stress. This research extends the concept to the subnational level: the feedback between individual local governments (communes) and the banks most concentrated in lending to them. The two transmission channels below operate simultaneously but at different scales and with different data requirements.

Two Transmission Channels

Channel 1 · Primary Research Focus
Domestic Local-Government Channel
Luxembourg banks extend direct credit to communes — project loans, revolving facilities, and bond subscriptions. When a commune's fiscal position deteriorates, the exposed bank faces credit risk concentration: a single borrower (the commune) accounts for a disproportionate share of a smaller bank's public-sector book.

This channel is the original contribution of the research design. It requires bank-by-commune exposure data (BCL supervisory, MOU required). The key question is whether commune-level fiscal stress Granger-causes changes in credit supply from the exposed institution — a testable, falsifiable causal claim.

Operationalisation: Herfindahl–Hirschman Index (HHI) of commune credit per bank; threshold events (commune deficit streak ≥ 2 years); credit growth differentials in exposed vs. unexposed banks.
Channel 2 · Contextual / Documented
Euro-Area Sovereign Bond Channel
Luxembourg MFIs hold approximately €64 bn in EA sovereign bonds (primarily French, Belgian, Italian and Spanish), representing ~6.5% of total assets. This is the dominant transmission channel for Luxembourg as a financial hub: EA sovereign stress impairs bank balance sheets through mark-to-market losses and collateral haircuts.

This channel is well-documented in the existing literature (Acharya et al. 2014; De Grauwe & Ji 2013) and is visible in the ECB BSI data in this platform. It provides important context but is not the original contribution of this research — it is the backdrop against which the subnational domestic channel operates.

Role in the proposal: Establishes why Luxembourg banks are already exposed to sovereign risk at the EA level, making the additional subnational domestic exposure analytically meaningful.

Why the Subnational Dimension Matters

National fiscal aggregates mask substantial heterogeneity across Luxembourg's 102 communes. Luxembourg City accounts for roughly 30% of the country's population and generates a large share of local tax revenue. Smaller rural communes — particularly in the Oesling (north) — depend on intergovernmental transfers for 40–60% of revenues, have ageing populations, and face structurally higher per-capita expenditure pressures.

The national aggregate fiscal position (surplus ~1–3% GDP) is therefore a poor guide to the fiscal risk profile of individual communes. An ML model trained only on national indicators would miss this heterogeneity entirely. The subnational panel is the key enabling dataset.

§5 ML Early-Warning System — Research Design Research Design / Prototype Pipeline

This section specifies the machine-learning pipeline for H3. No model has been trained yet — this is a pre-registered design, developed before data access, following best practice for computational social science reproducibility. The pipeline architecture follows the same pattern used in AreaStat AT, a companion regional fiscal analysis platform, and is adapted to the Luxembourg commune-bank context.

ℹ Model status — three distinct layers: (1) Heuristic baseline (implemented): deterministic 4-component stress index, visible in the Evidence panel and served live by the API. (2) Prototype panel (built): 102-commune socioeconomic panel with proxy distress labels — useful for verifying pipeline mechanics only, not for estimating true fiscal distress. (3) Supervised ML (planned WP3): XGBoost + SHAP model trained on labelled fiscal events — not started; requires commune financial accounts and BCL supervisory data.
⚠ Proxy label warning: The current panel_dataset.csv uses socioeconomic indicators (unemployment rate, income index, employment rate) as a proxy for fiscal distress. This label is useful for testing pipeline mechanics and feature engineering workflows — it is not a measure of fiscal distress and should not be interpreted as one. It will be replaced by binary events derived from commune financial accounts (STATEC) and bank exposure data (BCL) in WP1.

SHAP Interpretability Design

SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) TreeExplainer will be applied to the trained XGBoost model to produce two layers of interpretability:

  • Global: Mean |SHAP value| per feature group — identifies which dimensions (fiscal, economic, demographic, bank exposure) drive the model's predictions across the full commune panel.
  • Local (per-commune): Top-3 SHAP drivers for each flagged commune in a given year — actionable explanation for macroprudential reporting.

The FastAPI endpoint /shap_explain will return per-commune SHAP vectors for integration into this frontend (planned in WP3).

§6 Empirical Strategy

The empirical programme tests three nested hypotheses using a commune-year panel for Luxembourg. The estimation strategy scales with data availability: a minimum viable design uses publicly available STATEC/Eurostat aggregates; the full design adds BCL supervisory bank-commune exposure data.

Research Design Matrix

HypothesisOutcomeKey Treatment / PredictorModelDecision Criterion
H1 Subnational nexus Credit growth or contraction to commune borrowers Lagged bank exposure concentration to fiscal-stress communes Bank-commune-year fixed effects regression Negative, significant exposure coefficient after bank, commune, and year FE
H2 Fiscal-credit causality Future bank credit supply to commune i Lagged fiscal stress indicators and deficit persistence Panel VAR / Granger tests / event study Fiscal indicators improve prediction of credit contraction beyond credit lags
H3 ML early warning Fiscal stress at t+1 or t+2 Fiscal, demographic, spatial, and bank-exposure feature groups Logit baseline, random forest, XGBoost + SHAP Out-of-sample AUC-ROC, PR-AUC, Brier score, and false-alarm rate beat heuristic baseline

Identification Challenges

Data Availability
Commune fiscal accounts and bank-commune credit registers are not publicly available. The minimum viable design uses STATEC aggregates; the full design requires two separate MOU negotiations.
Endogeneity
Fiscal stress and credit contraction are simultaneous: banks may tighten credit in anticipation of fiscal deterioration, biasing OLS estimates of the nexus coefficient.
Reverse Causality
Credit contraction (H1) may cause fiscal stress (H2) rather than the reverse. Granger causality tests and instrumental variable designs address this, but require sufficient panel depth.
Spatial Autocorrelation
Communes in the same canton face correlated shocks. Standard errors must be clustered at the canton level or modelled explicitly via spatial lag / spatial error specifications.
Small Sample
102 communes × 14 years = ~1,400 commune-years. Some stress events will be rare, creating class imbalance for ML and limited power for IV designs.

Identification Strategies

Lagged Instruments
Use HHIb,i,t-2 (two-year lag) as instrument for current-year bank exposure concentration, conditional on commune and bank fixed effects.
Pre-sample Specialisation
Banks' pre-sample (2008–2009) share of local-government credit as instrument for current exposure — exogenous to commune fiscal outcomes during the study period.
Event Study Design
Compare communes with ≥2 consecutive deficits vs. matched controls, examining credit availability in the two years surrounding the onset of the deficit streak.
Spatial Econometrics
Spatial lag model (SLM) and spatial error model (SEM) to test whether H1 effects propagate across commune borders — identifying the geographic scope of the nexus.
Subnational Vulnerability Explorer — Luxembourg Communes
Socio-economic indicators across 102 Luxembourg communes (LAU 2021). This map provides the spatial context for H1 and H3: commune-level heterogeneity in income, employment, and demographics is the structural foundation of subnational fiscal risk differentiation.
